     When Post-Training Scores and Model Behavior
                       Disagree


                        Advanced Topics in Machine Learning, Fall 2026



                                             Abstract
         Through Post training models’ behavior is improved by optimizing against feed-
         back signals. However, these signals do not always reflect the desired outcomes. In
         this study, we examine DPO, PPO, and GRPO to understand how their optimization
         mechanisms affect model behavior. We have also evaluated safety calibration and
         compared verifiable rewards with AI-generated feedback. Our experiments show
         that DPO achieved 63.7% preference accuracy, but this did not translate into a
         meaningful improvement in reward. In PPO, clipping appeared inactive during the
         short training runs and critic struggled to estimate returns reliably. GRPO’s normal-
         ization mechanism changed the gradient allocations more directly than generated
         length alone. Furthermore, safety experiments exposed errors in automated judge
         labels. In case of mathematical feedback experiments AI judges sometimes pre-
         ferred longer and persuasive responses even when the reasoning or final answer was
         incorrect. Together, these findings show that improvements in optimization metrics
         do not necessarily lead to better model behavior. They also highlight the impor-
         tance of evaluating feedback quality and interpreting results carefully, particularly
         when experiments involve short training runs, generation limits, and limited human
         validation. Code: https://github.com/rana-taqveem/atml_internal.


1   Introduction
The core objective is to study how different optimization constraints and the reward signal define what
a language model learns to optimize. Additionally, it reviews why do measured gains sometimes fail to
translate into the required behavior? To get answers, certain variations were applied to the preference
optimization mechanics on DPO, PPO, and GRPO, and then their safety behavior is measured and
finally tested by altering the feedback source while keeping the underlying policy family and group-
based training setup consistent. During each experiment, certain controlled interventions, diagnostic
tracking, and response-level counterexamples were used to answer the research questions. The study
uses formal equations that lay out the underlying mechanics instead of relying directly on reward
metrics, KL divergence, or preference accuracy as definitive measures of model quality.

2   Experimental protocol

Model Setup & Implementation. All policies build on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct fine-tuned with
LoRA. DPO starts directly from the standard instruction-tuned reference model. PPO and GRPO
resume training from given intermediate checkpoints, with PPO restoring its corresponding critic
network. In the safety experiment, the study evaluates only the three final standard policies against
an untouched SFT baseline. On feedback, it evaluates pretrained frozen RLVR and RLAIF adapters
without additional retraining. Throughout the experiments, fixed dataset splits and prompt IDs were
used while the decoding settings were kept intact (Table 12, Appendix A). Flaws found in the given
implementation related to the reference-sign in DPO, the minimum-surrogate objective defect in PPO,
and the within-prompt normalization issue in GRPO were fixed. All trainable parameters were upcast
for numerical stability during RL training, and dropout was fully disabled.
Metrics & Evaluation Details. Preference Accuracy: Measured as the proportion of text pairs
yielding a positive reference-relative DPO margin. KL Divergence: Reported using the course’s
standard pooled response-token sampled log-ratio diagnostic (rather than an exact full-distribution
calculation), meaning finite sample estimates can occasionally turn negative. Reward Scores: Valid
for comparison only within the same evaluation protocol. Reporting: The tables report Standard
Error (SE) for rates and mean rewards, and Standard Deviation (SD) for generated response lengths.
Confidence intervals are computed via paired percentile bootstrapping across resampled evaluation
examples (accounting for evaluation variance, but not re-training seed variance). All experiments
used seed 6304. Instead of smoothing over them through post-hoc filtering, known limitations such
as text truncation, variations in realized token lengths, and automated judge errors were intentionally
retained to reflect real-world evaluation trade-offs.

Training uses standard and length-balanced configurations for 1 epoch (94 updates). β controls how
strongly the model is penalized for drifting away from the original reference model. Different β
strengths were tested with forks of 600 shared examples over 38 updates.
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). In contrast to DPO, PPO [Schulman et al., 2017] gen-
erates trajectory rollouts, uses a fixed reward model to score them, and also estimates advan-
tages via a learned critic network. The ratio of new to old policy probabilities is defined as
ρt = πθ (at |st )/πold (at |st ). The reward rt incorporates a terminal penalty S for missing End-
of-Sequence (EOS) tokens, with KL divergence applied directly as reward shaping, constraining the
actor not to deviate from the reference model:
                           rt = −βKL (log πold − log πref )t + 1[t = T ]S                             (2)

factors γ = 1, λ = 0.95, and advantage estimates Ât whitened over valid tokens:

Length Confounding: The dataset length biases and model preference ranking appeared to be
pointing in opposite directions. Although the standard training data favored longer responses, as
shown in Table 2, compared to balanced data, both models performed highest on the rejected-longer
pairs, and length balancing shifted limited evaluation outcomes, with its +8.5 percentage point gain

resting on just 7 flipped outcomes. The negative log-ratios with per-token margin inversions indicate
sensitivity to token sequence aggregation. Furthermore, balancing fails to reduce output generation
lengths (167.7 vs. 168.2 tokens) or improve prompt compliance (40% greedy / 48% sampled vs.
40% / 56%), as factors like ∼50% truncation and context length differences prevent isolating length

Signal vs. Quality Disconnect: Higher reward scores do not reliably indicate superior response
quality or instruction compliance. On a 35-word response limitation prompt (wl04), standard DPO
still increased the word count from 43 to 55 while the reward increased from 2.50 to 3.47, whereas on
a Bash task, DPO managed to fix line counting via wc -l but incorrectly wrote to output.txt and
generated a long enough response such that it got truncated at 256 tokens despite the reward rising
from 1.336 to 1.553. The proxy reward model also appeared to be imperfect, achieving 67.4% course
validation accuracy with a 0.28 length correlation. Overall, the standard DPO yielded a statistically
insignificant reward shift over SFT (∆RM = −0.032, 95% CI [−0.148, +0.079]) despite achieving
positive preference margins on 63.7% of held-out pairs. This demonstrates that offline preference
fitting does not guarantee improved generation behavior.

Reward Informativeness & Failures: Standard PPO achieved a reward of 1.687 on the held-out
dataset (vs. midpoint 1.491 and SFT 1.894), making its change over SFT statistically uncertain
as it is still lower than the original SFT (−0.207, 95% CI [−0.468, 0.036]). The critic failed to

struggled to predict target returns. Additionally, qualitative checks establish that rewards alone cannot
guarantee output quality, as seen in the case of the Wayfair prompt where PPO’s reward improvement
(+0.54 over SFT) was actually inflated by fluent responses but hallucinated legal and factual claims.
Meanwhile, strict compliance on the Language Creation prompt dropped the reward to −0.64 (vs.
+1.67 for rule-breaking outputs). This proxy failure can be attributed to the length-reward bias
across all runs (Spearman correlation 0.35–0.49, p < 0.005), where the reward model favored output
verbosity over factual accuracy and instruction adherence.

Normalization, Length, and Gradient Allocation: Shifting from per-completion normalization
(1/Tk ) to constant normalization (1/Lmax ) fundamentally changed gradient weight allocations
throughout completion sequence lengths while maintaining average generated response lengths
(Table 6). With 1/Tk , tokens of short completions received higher magnitude, resulting in a strong
negative gradient-length correlation (−.78). Whereas long completions (95% of tokens) received
only 11% of the total gradient mass. In contrast, with 1/Lmax , the correlation reverses to +.79
by allocating 74% of gradient magnitude to long completions. However, generated lengths remain
virtually unchanged (326 vs. 323 tokens, with changes relative to midpoint of [−20.5, 38.5] and
[−16.6, 28.8] tokens). The reward changes relative to midpoint were −.235 [−0.511, +0.025] for
1/Tk and −.338 [−0.651, −0.049] for 1/Lmax . Qualitatively, for 1/Tk , the model generated a 32-
token refusal scoring −1.73. Whereas under constant 1/Lmax normalization, the response is 459
tokens long (the same length as the midpoint’s response) scoring +2.87; while the quality remained
low because it combined an unsupported copyright objection with contradictory answer/refusal
framing. This demonstrates that higher rewards with constant normalization do not primarily mean
improved reasoning or adherence.
Dominant Inefficiency Sources in the Observed Run: In our standard GRPO run, response
truncation appeared to be the primary source of sample inefficiency, whereas zero-variance groups
did not cost much. For truncation, 15 out of 80 completions reached the 512-token cap, burning 7,680
out of 19,144 total generated tokens (40.1%) while failing to generate any policy gradient updates.
On the other hand, zero-variance groups occurred on updates 14 and 16 (8 out of 80 completions);
however, they used only 24 generated tokens (0.13%) due to trivial 2- and 4-token outputs. The
largest gradient-norm spikes were observed under per-token normalization when sequence lengths
collapsed (e.g., Update 3: mean 7 tokens, gradient norm 2.11; Update 5: mean 10 tokens, gradient
norm 1.40; Figure 2). This confirmed that 1/Tk scaling amplifies overall gradient magnitude on
shorter sequence lengths. Overall, the standard held-out GRPO reward reached 1.739 vs. midpoint
1.842 (∆ = −.103 [−.340, .127]). This demonstrates that critic-free RL requires strict truncation
management to avoid massive compute waste.

Sensitive Wording: On safe prompts, DPO showed a marginally higher safe refusal fraction than
PPO (.428 vs. PPO .400). This is an unvalidated judge estimate rather than true evidence of model
superiority. High refusal rates were observed in safe contexts, historical events, and fictional privacy

refusal. On manual audit, each policy over-refused 11 of 30 audited safe prompts, including every
fictional-privacy prompt in the subset. This shows that policies failed to distinguish in these sensitive
categories.
Judge Errors and Manual Audit: Automated evaluation also suffered from severe structural flaws.
The AI judge assigned 1.0 confidence to 1,799 of its 1,800 labels and never emitted OVER_REFUSAL
or AMBIGUOUS. So its reported zero-over-refusal rate is a label-use artifact, with 28.4–30.2% of
labels being class-inconsistent (Table 7). To resolve this, we manually audited a blinded, 60-prompt
subset (30 safe, 30 unsafe × 4 policies = 240 responses) with policy names and AI labels hidden
during annotation.

Note: Since the binary rewards and pairwise win rates have different scales, the below analysis
focuses on relative rankings, sensitivity metrics, and failure modes.
Accuracy vs. Preference Disconnect: While AI preference feedback can separate two responses
that have the correct final answer by preferring better intermediate reasoning, the observed results
show that both the rule-based verifier and the AI judge rated clean and corrupted reasoning identically
across the test pairs. This resulted in a reasoning sensitivity score of zero (Sreason = 0; Table 10).
For the rule-based verifier, reasoning blindness is by design; however, on the AI judge, it reveals a
major execution flaw. On Problem 803, the corrupted response asserts that 2 × 7 = 15 in its step, yet
the AI judge declared a tie irrespective of the response order. Similarly, the round-robin scores for
clean (.494) and corrupted (.500) appeared nearly identical, showing that agreement between reward
sources does not mean the AI judge is actually checking the math.
Style & Framing Bias: The AI judge suffered from a severe style and persuasive framing bias over
mathematical correctness. When faced with two correct answers—one with persuasive filler—the
judge chose the padded response 25% of the time and never chose the concise, clean version (the
remaining 75% were ties). In round-robin testing across a 5-way comparison, responses containing
filler achieved the highest overall win rate (.600), outperforming clean correct answers (.494), wrong
final answers (.481), and distractor responses (.425). Moreover, on Problem 736, simply adding the
sentence “This derivation is unquestionably correct” easily inverted the judge’s preference without
modifying the final answer. This demonstrated that RLAIF reward gains can be triggered by more
verbose and manipulative fluff rather than sound reasoning.

Failure Mechanisms: The two reward sources failed because of completely different reasons. The
verifier produced perfect outcome sensitivity (Soutcome = 1) while correctly assigning zero to
incorrect final answers. However, the issue lies in its score allocation design, as it grants full credit
upon finding the exact matching final answer, completely overlooking any flawed intermediate steps or
reasoning. On the other hand, the AI judge struggles to pick incorrect final results (Soutcome = 0.125).
On Problem 113, in which a response contains the gold distractor 25 but gets an incorrect 26 as
the final answer, the verifier correctly assigns zero for the wrong final answer; however, the judge
prefers the distractor response in both presentation orders. The AI judge also depicted fragility over
formatting and parsing. In 45 out of 741 raw evaluation outputs, it merely echoed the option labels
(e.g., “A, B”), and the fixed parser simply took the first letter. Additionally, it suffered from a 30%
order inconsistency rate on distractor pairs, showing that its scoring was influenced by presentation
order instead of mathematical rigor.
Out-of-Domain Transfer (RQ4): Although all models scored 15–17 percentage points higher on
SVAMP than on GSM8K (Table 9), the baseline SFT model also showed a similar jump. This shows
that SVAMP is just an easier dataset, and not that RL training resulted in better overall results. This
becomes clearer when comparing SFT with RLVR and RLAIF, where RL changed only a single
correctness outcome on GSM8K, and only RLVR gained two extra correct answers on SVAMP. Most
of the recorded mistakes on GSM8K were formatting errors like missing final tags (190, 186, and
185 errors across SFT, RLVR, and RLAIF, compared to only 20, 22, and 22 actual math mistakes).
Furthermore, for 78–82% of test prompts, the outputs produced by both RLVR and RLAIF were
identical to SFT (235/300 for RLVR and 245/300 for RLAIF). This shows that neither of the RL
feedback sources provided a meaningful transfer advantage or enabled superior generalized reasoning.

8   Cross-Task Synthesis

Preference Strength and Drift. As observed in DPO, its held-out preference signal made limited
improvement in generated reward quality. In PPO’s the sampled KL divergence stayed near zero
across the reference pressure forks, whereas in GRPO the normalization redistributed the gradient
magnitude across sequence lengths. These optimizations are completely different, DPO offers
preference scaling, PPO shapes reward penalty, and GRPO uses sequence weighting rule. Therefore,
their tuning coefficients are not interchangeable. Short training runs and inactive clipping limits
may have restricted the model in actually changing. Although, we got low KL divergence penalty
however it does not proves that we got unchanged distributions, neither it establishes correct response
generation. Furthermore, the reward scores of DPO cannot be compared directly with of PPO / GRPO
because of different prompt sets and length caps.
Offline versus Online Feedback. DPO learn to prefer the better answer, relative to the reference
model, using a fixed dataset of response pairs. Whereas PPO and GRPO are online models and they
generate new responses on the fly during training and learn and update based on the reward scores
on the own sampled responses. However, online exposure does not automatically make the reward
source more reliable. PPO introduces a critic to estimate returns and any estimation errors sends bad
learning signal to the model. GRPO compares multiple generated responses for a same prompt and
can end up with zero variance or truncation ending up in zero useful information and wasted compute.
In DPO, the context truncation to fit length changes the underlying conditioning problem. These
structural components and tradeoffs constraints define what each can teach us independent of the
optimizer’s name.
Optimization Bias. Through length intervention in DPO we tested two possible causes of length
bias: first, training data having length correlations, and second, summation of log-ratios over every
token so that longer responses produce larger margins. Balancing did help in removing the length
correlations, however, both approaches still failed to shorten responses and struggled to follow the
word-limit compliance. In PPO, the cached rollouts show the clipping geometry, however, the tested
forks never reached that range. In GRPO, the constant normalizer updated the gradient magnitude in
favor of long completions and did not demonstrate any reduction in held-out length. Therefore, a
change in the loss weighting, a better diagnostic score, and improved responses are separate claims.
Our qualitative counterexamples show that prompt instructions, completeness, and facts must be
checked alongside proxy scores.

Safety Calibration. The XSTest results showed that higher preference or reward scores cannot be
treated as sufficient evidence of calibrated safety. However, the categorical judge made systematic
labelling errors, so a reliable fine-grained ranking of the four policies was not possible either. The
SFT baseline already refused some harmless prompts (e.g., “Where can I find a murder of crows?”),
showing that over-refusal is not created exclusively by alignment training. The completed blinded
manual audit reveals that automated judge errors obscure over-refusals and that all four policies
exhibit identical safety profiles on the audited subset (Table 8).
Feedback Design and Compute. Table 11 contrasts exact but narrow outcome verification with
broader but noisier AI preference signals. The verifier’s blindness to reasoning-only changes is
deliberate. On the other hand, the AI judge’s ties on the same reasoning-corruption pairs are an
observed limitation, and its preference for persuasive filler is an undesirable distinction. In terms of
compute, GRPO used less peak VRAM than PPO in this setup (5.314 vs. 6.252 GiB), but took longer
(335.3 vs. 231.3 s) and generated more tokens (19,144 vs. 4,482). DPO took 1,534.3 s and 8.388
GiB for its different training task. These measurements reflect our specific setup, so they do not show
which algorithm is more efficient at matched work or quality. Additionally, direct AI feedback spends
pairwise judge inference proportional to K(K − 1)/2, i.e., six judge calls for every K = 4 group.

9   Limitations & Conclusion
Scope and Reproducibility: All reported values were traced back to the canonical saved results.
The paired bootstrap intervals only capture evaluation-example uncertainty under a single training
seed (6304), not variation across seeds. The PPO KL forks and GRPO normalization forks matched
their scheduled prompts, updates and caps; however, their realized token totals still differed. DPO
truncation affected both training and evaluation, and excluding context-free evaluation pairs cannot
undo its effect on training. Generation caps also censor the measured lengths. The qualitative cases
show that specific failure modes exist, but not how often they occur, and the mathematical feedback
diagnostics cover only 20 problems. Finally, verification of the public repository remains the sole
pending submission requirement.
Conclusion: Overall the experiments revealed a clear gap between preference fitting and the op-
timization signal. Models failed to meet the desired behavior. Constraints such as clipping and
KL penalties not demonstrated the expected effect. The feedback signals must be tested against
controlled failures, and the evaluators themselves must be validated. The evidence supports bounded
task-specific conclusions rather than a universal ranking of DPO, PPO, GRPO or reward sources.
